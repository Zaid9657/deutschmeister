## Single spots

### S1 — MAJOR, L10 scene S5/dictation: changing platform is taught as changing trains

**Text:** “Durchsage: Der Zug hat Verspätung. Bitte an Gleis fünf umsteigen!” (`src/data/curricula/a11.js:2372`; `scene-a1.1-l10-5`; dictation line 9). `umsteigen` means changing vehicles/trains; the sequence suggests a changed platform after an earlier departure from Gleis vier. It never supplies the transfer train or makes clear which event is occurring. The new comprehension key “The train is delayed; please change at platform five.” perpetuates the ambiguity. A learner using this model at a station may misunderstand which action is required.

**Smallest correction:** choose one event. If this is a platform change, use “Der Zug hat Verspätung. Er fährt heute von Gleis fünf.” and update the dependent question/dictation. If a transfer is intended, provide the second train/destination and an actual transfer instruction. Rule: an announcement’s action, destination and checked English meaning must describe the same event.

### S2 — MAJOR, L10 scene S4/fluency: one answer attaches to two unrelated questions

**Text:** “Fährt der Bus auch in die Schweiz? Ist das Gleis weit?” / “Nein, leider nicht. Die Abfahrt ist hier an Gleis vier.” (`a11.js:2365`–2366; `scene-a1.1-l10-4`). The meaning question asks only whether the platform is far and is answerable from `hier`; that does not resolve what “Nein, leider nicht” answers. The learner repeats both unrelated questions as one fluency turn. The writer’s doubt is confirmed by the current built lines.

**Smallest correction:** make the pair one question/one answer about the platform, and leave the bus-to-Switzerland fact in the read where it is explicit. Reuse only the coherent question as the speaking target. Rule: a chunk with one checked interpretation must make the reference of its answer unambiguous.

### S3 — MAJOR, L9 scene S4: “ein Brot” is translated as a sandwich

**Text:** Ana says “Nein, danke. Ich esse ein Brot mit Salat.” (`a11.js:2166`); the dialogue’s English is “No, thank you. I’ll have a sandwich with salad.” The new meaning check correctly uses bread with salad, so the learner now has two different English interpretations for the same food order. `ein Brot` does not reliably mean a prepared sandwich; at a café this wording is an unsafe model for the intended order.

**Smallest correction:** keep the taught vocabulary and say “Nein, danke. Ich möchte Brot und Salat, bitte.” with matching English and dependent checks, or explicitly teach a sandwich/belegtes Brot if that is the intended food. Rule: a food-order gloss must name the item the German order would obtain.

### S4 — MAJOR, L2 scene S5/fluency: an incomplete phone prefix is called a full number

**Text:** “Meine Telefonnummer ist null eins sieben sechs.” (`a11.js:587`; `scene-a1.1-l02-5`); answer `0176`. Four digits are a useful beginning-number exercise but not a plausible complete German mobile contact number. The learner is asked to repeat the entire claim, and the can-do promises their phone number. The later writing form in L9 supplies a longer number, without resolving the earlier model.

**Smallest correction:** label it explicitly as the start of a number, or give a complete fictional example after teaching the digits and split it into manageable listening units. Rule: numbers called complete contact numbers must fit the situation; shortened examples must say they are shortened.

### S5 — MINOR, L1 personal spelling: cue and target name disagree

**Text:** after entering one’s surname, the say/fluency cue still reads “Buchstabieren Sie bitte Chakiri.” (`a11.js:289`; `buildGuided.js:18`; `SceneStage.jsx:51`; `FluencyStage.jsx:16`), while `sayText` spells the learner’s surname (`scene.js:7`). This can make an isolated learner doubt which name to say; it does not remove the ability to complete the task.

**Smallest correction:** substitute the entered surname into the personal cue or use a neutral “Buchstabieren Sie bitte Ihren Nachnamen.” Rule: personalized prompts and targets must refer to the same person/value.

### S6 — MINOR, L9 scene S5: the final spoken line has no conversational reason

**Text:** Paul: “Gut. Die Kellnerin kommt sofort mit dem Brot.” Ana: “Danke! Zum Frühstück trinke ich immer eine Tasse Kaffee.” (`a11.js:2167`–2168; `scene-a1.1-l09-5`). German is grammatical; the sudden breakfast habit is not a natural response to the service update. This is the learner’s compulsory say-line and fluency repetition, so two speaking turns rehearse a weak exchange.

**Smallest correction:** a normal acknowledgement/order-closing line here; teach Frühstück/Tasse through the menu or an appropriate breakfast exchange. Rule: the target response must perform a sensible next turn in the supplied situation.

### S7 — MINOR, L10 notice: rising intonation is stated too absolutely

**Text:** “Die Satzmelodie steigt am Ende.” / “your voice goes up at the end of the question” (`a11.js:2384`–2385). Rising melody is a useful neutral beginner model for yes/no questions, but not an invariant German rule. Combined with all three arrow questions keyed to ↗, this risks teaching that a natural falling yes/no question is incorrect.

**Smallest correction:** say it is a common neutral model, and supply verified audio for that model. Rule: present a pronunciation pattern as a model where context permits alternatives; do not grade the alternative as a grammatical impossibility.
